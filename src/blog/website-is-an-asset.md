A website is not a subscription.

It is a business asset, treated by the tax code the same way as inventory, equipment, or insurance. The mistake most small business owners make is comparing the monthly fee to other monthly fees: the gym membership, the streaming service, the software subscription they never use. That comparison is the wrong category and the wrong instinct. It leads to canceling a thing that was making money.

Here is the accounting reality, the tax reality, and the equity reality. None of it is invented.

## The tax reality

A managed website at $150/month is $1,800 a year. That entire $1,800 is a fully deductible ordinary business expense. It reduces your taxable income dollar-for-dollar. At a 25% effective tax rate, the after-tax cost of the website is $1,350. At higher rates, less.

Your accountant categorizes it under advertising, marketing, or professional services depending on your bookkeeping conventions. There is no special election or paperwork. The deduction is automatic on Schedule C (sole proprietor) or your business return (LLC, S-Corp, C-Corp).

For sites built as a one-time project over $2,500, the IRS may require capitalization and amortization over 36 to 60 months. This is a paperwork issue, not a value-of-the-deduction issue. Most small businesses on a $150/mo managed plan never hit the capitalization threshold because the monthly fee is treated as ongoing service, not capital investment. Your accountant decides which category fits.

The cheaper version of this idea: every dollar you spend on the website saves you 20 to 35 cents in taxes you would have paid anyway.

## The 8826 angle

This one is underused.

IRS Form 8826 is the Disabled Access Credit. Small businesses (under $1 million in revenue OR fewer than 30 full-time employees) can claim a tax credit equal to 50% of eligible accessibility-related expenses, up to $5,000 per year.

Critical word: credit. Not deduction. A deduction reduces taxable income. A credit reduces the tax owed, dollar-for-dollar.

What qualifies as "accessibility-related" includes website improvements that meet WCAG accessibility standards. <a href="/blog/wcag-2-2-aa-in-five-minutes/">WCAG 2.2 AA is the standard most ADA lawsuits cite.</a> Building an accessible website, or remediating an existing one, falls inside the credit.

The math, for a small business: spend $2,000 on accessibility work, claim $1,000 credit (50%), reduce your tax bill by $1,000. The first $2,000 of accessibility-related website spend each year is effectively half-priced.

Most small business owners do not know this credit exists. Most accountants do not raise it unless asked. The credit has been on the books since 1990. It is real, used, and dramatically underclaimed.

We are not tax advisors. Ask yours. Bring up Form 8826 specifically.

## The equity reality

A website is one of the few assets a small business builds that has a measurable, transferable value separate from the owner.

Three things accumulate value over time:

**Search rankings.** Google's algorithm rewards age, consistency, and continued maintenance. A site that has ranked for "plumber in Plano" for three years ranks higher than a brand new site with the same content. The rankings are an asset. They cannot be bought separately. They are not transferable to a different domain. They are tied to the site.

**Email list.** Every visitor who hands over their email address is a captured lead that does not require re-paying Google or Facebook to reach. The list grows continuously while the site is live. Mid-size service businesses we work with hit 500 to 2,000 captured emails by year two. At $0.50 to $2.00 per email of replacement cost (the rough price of acquiring an equivalent lead through paid ads), that list is worth $1,000 to $4,000 of latent value.

**Brand recognition in search.** "Brand search" is the number of people who Google your business name directly because they have heard of you. It grows with every billboard, every referral, every social post, every news mention. The website is the destination that captures the value of all that branded interest. Without the site, the brand search hits a dead end.

When a business is sold, these three things are part of the valuation. Two businesses with identical revenue and identical owners can sell at 20–30% different multiples based on whether the website is "their working lead engine" or "an old WordPress install nobody touches." Brokers know this. Owners often do not.

## The right comparisons

Stop comparing the website monthly to Netflix.

The right comparisons:

**Inventory.** A retail business buys $1,800 worth of inventory and accepts that not all of it will sell immediately. The website is the same: a stocked asset that produces revenue over the year, not all at once.

**Equipment.** A landscaping business buys a $1,800 piece of equipment and uses it for years. The website is the same: an asset with a useful life of several years before it needs major refresh.

**Insurance.** A business pays $1,800 a year for liability coverage hoping never to claim it. The website is the inverse: $1,800 a year to make sure customers can find you, with claims (leads) you expect to use every month.

The website fits all three comparisons. It fits none of the "monthly subscription" comparisons.

## What this changes about the decision

If you treat the website as a recurring expense competing with your other recurring expenses, you cancel it the first quarter cash gets tight. The site goes down. The traffic stops. The email list goes stale. The rankings drift. The equity you built evaporates.

If you treat it as an asset, you protect it the same way you protect your inventory, your equipment, your insurance. You renew it. You maintain it. You measure its return. You write off the cost. You claim every credit it qualifies for.

That accounting move is the single highest-leverage thing a small business owner can do for the website. Not the design. Not the SEO. The framing.

Reply with the monthly category you currently put your website under in your books. If it is "subscriptions" or "miscellaneous," it is in the wrong column. Move it to "advertising" or "marketing" and most of the second-guessing goes with it.
