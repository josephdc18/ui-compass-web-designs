The most common reason small business owners delay getting a website is not the cost.

It is the fear of having to manage it.

The fear is justified for some setups and completely wrong for others. The difference is how the site is built and who is responsible for it after launch. Here is what management actually involves, what the right setup makes invisible, and what the wrong setup quietly costs you.

## What "managing a website" actually means

There are two completely separate categories of work. Most owners conflate them.

**Category A: Background maintenance.** This is the work that keeps the site functioning. Security patches. Hosting renewals. SSL certificates. Plugin updates. Uptime monitoring. Database backups. Performance monitoring. Broken-link cleanup. Image optimization on uploads. Form-delivery testing. Mobile rendering checks. None of it is visible to the visitor. All of it is required.

**Category B: Content work.** This is the work that keeps the site current. New blog posts. Updated prices. A new staff bio. A swapped photo. A fresh testimonial. Adding a new service. Updating the hours during holiday weeks. This is the work owners think of when they hear "manage a website."

Most owners worry about Category A but actually spend their time on Category B. Both can be outsourced. Both have different cost structures.

## What it looks like on a managed site

Category A: zero owner time. The maintenance is included in the monthly. The owner does not know it happened. The notifications go to us. The patches happen on the schedule we choose. The site stays up, secure, fast, and indexed without anyone asking.

Category B: minutes per request. The owner emails or messages what they want changed. We change it, often the same day. The owner does not log into anything, does not learn a CMS, does not pick fonts, does not wrestle with the breakpoint that looks wrong on mobile. They send the request, we ship the change.

Total monthly owner time: 30 to 60 minutes for a typical small business. The bulk of that is composing the requests.

The trade-off is the $150 monthly fee. That fee is the price of making all of Category A and most of Category B invisible.

## What it looks like on a self-managed Squarespace, Wix, or WordPress site

Category A: more than you think. Even Squarespace and Wix do not handle everything for you. Plugin updates, font deprecations, image format changes, form integration bugs, content backups, and the occasional theme-breaking update all land on the owner. WordPress is the worst here. Squarespace is the best, but still not zero.

Plan for 2 to 4 hours a month of Category A on a Squarespace site. More on WordPress. Less on Wix because Wix locks down so much that you cannot break it. (You also cannot extend it.)

Category B: every change is yours. You log in. You find the page. You navigate the editor. You make the change. You preview it on desktop, then mobile. You publish. You verify it actually went live. You discover the change broke a layout somewhere else. You fix that. You verify again.

A simple page edit (swap a paragraph, change a photo, update a price) usually takes 15 to 30 minutes the first time. After a year of doing it, maybe 10. Times the number of edits per month.

Total monthly owner time on self-managed: usually 3 to 8 hours. Sometimes more. Sometimes a weekend.

At $50/hr opportunity cost: $150 to $400/month worth of your time.

The Squarespace fee was $20/month. The actual cost was the $20 plus your $200 in time. Total: $220/month for a slower site you maintain yourself.

The managed alternative was $150/month for a faster site you do not maintain at all. The math favors hiring it out for most owners.

## The myth: "but I want control"

This is the objection that ends most sales calls.

The owner says: "I want to be able to update it myself."

Two things to understand.

First, on a managed model you still control everything. You decide what changes. You decide what the homepage says. You decide which services to add. The difference is who types it into the editor. You direct, we execute. The control over content is yours either way.

Second, the owners we work with who came from self-managed setups almost always say, eighteen months in, that they do not miss the editor access. The first month feels strange. By month three, the relief of not seeing the platform login is real. By year two, the request-based model feels like the obvious way.

There is a small subset of owners who genuinely enjoy the editing work. For them, self-managed is the right call. We have clients in that camp on the build side (we wrote the code, they edit content themselves on a system that does not require them to maintain anything). It is rare.

## The myth: "edits will take forever"

The opposite is usually true.

On a managed site, edits typically ship the same business day. Most go out in two to four hours. The owner emails the change, we do it, we send a link to verify, the site is updated.

On a self-managed site, the edit goes out exactly as fast as the owner finds the time to make it. Owners who already work 60 hours a week often discover that "I'll update it this weekend" turns into eight weeks of an outdated price on the homepage.

The fastest edit cycle is usually the one where someone else makes the change for you. That is the part of the managed model that surprises owners most after they switch.

## What to ask any potential web partner

Three questions:

**What is included in the monthly?** If the answer is "hosting and one hour of edits," it is a different product than "unlimited edits and full management." Both are valid. Know what you are buying.

**How fast do edits typically ship?** Same day, two days, a week, by appointment. Ask for the typical timeline. Then ask for the longest typical timeline.

**What happens to the site if I stop paying?** If the answer is "the site goes offline," it is rented infrastructure. If the answer is "you keep the code and the domain, we just stop managing," it is a service. Both have trade-offs. The second one is what most small businesses should want.
