Web Development: Where Most Websites Fall Apart

Development is what separates a website that converts from one that just exists. There's a lot of misinformation out there about how websites are built. Anyone telling you that code doesn't matter anymore is either selling you something or doesn't know what they're talking about.

Right-click any website and pick "Inspect." You'll see thousands of lines of code. Every site on the internet runs on it. The question isn't whether code is involved, it's how well it's written.

You have a few choices when you need a website. Drag-and-drop builders like Wix or Squarespace are easy to start with, but they ship slow, bloated code that search engines struggle to read. WordPress can work well if you have a real budget and someone who knows what they're doing — but without that, sites end up duct-taped together with plugins that slow things down and stop getting security updates the moment something breaks.

Or you can work with us.

I get it. When you see a $200 website on Fiverr, or your friend's cousin who "knows a bit about computers" offers to put something together cheap, it's tempting. Really tempting. But here's what actually happens.

We've seen small businesses lose thousands in revenue because they tried to save a few hundred on their website.

Why Speed Matters

The reason we do things the way we do is to make sure your website loads fast. A website is just a bunch of files — code, images, fonts — served to someone through the internet. The smaller and tighter those files are, the faster the experience. Most of our pages are 2,000 to 3,000 lines of code. Compare that to 12,000+ on a typical WordPress or Squarespace site. All that extra weight is stuff we strip out from the start, while resizing, compressing, and optimizing every image and icon, and hosting the result on a global network so visitors get the site from a server close to them.

Less code to load means a faster experience. Every kilobyte saved is another fraction of a second your customer isn't waiting. A 2-second delay in load time can mean an 87% abandonment rate. Attention spans aren't getting longer, and you need every edge you can get.

Exceptional Website Security

A lot of websites are about as secure as leaving your front door wide open with a sign that says "valuables inside, please come in."

A website gets attacked every 39 seconds. Most sites are built on databases, plugins, and layered scripts that create more holes than swiss cheese.

Every plugin is a possible entry point. Every database connection is a vulnerability. Every "easy update" is one bad release away from breaking everything. And most of these attacks aren't even done by people — bots constantly scan the internet for sites running common plugins with unpatched issues. The thing is, most small businesses don't need a database or a backend at all. They need text and images on a screen, well-organized, with a clean design. All of that runs perfectly fine as a static website.

A static website is essentially impenetrable. No database to hack, no plugins to exploit, no admin panel to break into. Less worrying about your website means more time doing the work that actually pays the bills.

Website Accessibility

Over 61 million adults in the United States live with a disability, and many of them run into walls when using websites that weren't built with them in mind. Only 5.2% of the internet currently meets accessibility standards. Building an accessible site is the right thing to do, and on top of that it keeps you on the right side of the law.

Legal Implications of an Inaccessible Website

If your website isn't accessible, you could be breaking the law. Accessibility legislation has been expanding for years:

  • United States: The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) has been the basis for thousands of lawsuits against businesses with inaccessible sites. The legal fees from one of those cases usually cost more than building the site accessibly in the first place.
  • European Union: Any business serving EU customers has to comply with the European Accessibility Act, including WCAG 2.1 conformance.
  • United Kingdom: The Equality Act of 2010 requires sites and mobile apps to be perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust, with extra weight on public-sector entities.
  • Australia: The Disability Discrimination Act 1992 requires public-facing websites to be accessible.
  • Argentina: Law 26,653 requires both public and private websites to meet accessibility standards. No qualifications, no exceptions.

How to Make Your Website Accessible

  • Add alt text to every image so screen readers can describe what's on the page to visitors who are blind or partially sighted.
  • Add ARIA labels to links and buttons without text (a cart icon means nothing to a screen reader without a label).
  • Pick color combinations that work for colorblind users. Green text on a blue background, for example, isn't going to fly.
  • Use semantic heading levels (H1, H2, H3, and so on) so screen readers can announce the page structure correctly.
  • Keep a logical tab order that mirrors the menu, so the whole site is usable with just the keyboard.
  • Include breadcrumb navigation that reflects where the visitor is in the site.
  • Wherever you use bold or color to convey meaning, make sure the same information is still clear to assistive technologies.
