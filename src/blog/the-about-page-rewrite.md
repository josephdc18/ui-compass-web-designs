The About page is the second-most-visited page on most small business websites. It is also the page most often written for the wrong reader.

Open your analytics and look at the top five pages by traffic. The homepage is first, almost always. The About page is second on roughly four out of five small business sites we audit. The contact page is third. The service pages are scattered after that. Yet the About page is, on most sites, the least carefully written piece of content on the entire site. It is where the founder writes a small autobiography, the team gets a photo grid with smiles, and the visitor learns very little about why they should hire you.

This post is the rewrite. What an About page is actually for, the four sins that ruin most of them, and the three-block structure that converts.

## Who actually reads the About page

The visitor on the About page is in a specific mode. They have already glanced at the homepage. They have a working idea of what you do. Now they want to figure out whether you will be good for them. The mode is not curiosity — it is <span class="tooltip-term" data-tooltip="The pre-purchase research a customer does to confirm a business is real, competent, and a good fit before making contact or paying.">diligence</span>.

Three flavors of visitor land here.

### The referral visitor

A friend mentioned you. The visitor is checking whether you look legit before calling. Their question is, "is this person who my friend said they are?" The About page answers with real photos, named team members, and the kind of specifics that referral conversations include but websites usually leave out.

### The shortlist visitor

The visitor has three or four quotes on their desk. They are doing diligence on each business before deciding who to call back. The About page is doing comparison work — yours versus the other two. Their question is, "what makes this one different, and is the difference real?" The page that answers wins the call back.

### The pre-contract visitor

The visitor is about to sign a quote or a contract. They want one last look at who they are working with. The About page is the reassurance layer. Their question is, "am I making the right call?" A page that confirms reads as trustworthy; a page that disappoints undoes the homepage's work.

All three are asking variations of the same question: **will this business be good for me?** Not: who founded this business and when. The About page that mistakes the founder's biography for the answer fails all three visitors at the same time.

## The four sins of a typical About page

### Sin 1: Third-person voice

"Joseph C. founded UI Compass in 2020." Reads like a press release. Distances the visitor from the person they are about to hire.

The fix is first person on small business sites with a clear principal: "I founded UI Compass in 2020 because..." Same content, immediately warmer. The exception is businesses with multiple co-founders or no single principal — there, third-person can work, but the writing still has to feel like a human introducing themselves.

### Sin 2: Founder bio first

Almost every About page leads with the founder. Born in, graduated from, worked at, founded. The reader has to wait three paragraphs before the page answers the question they came with.

The fix is reordering. The visitor's question goes first. The founder's story comes later, framed by what it means for the customer.

### Sin 3: Stock photos of "professionals"

A team photo with smiling people staged in a conference room. A handshake over a laptop. A planning session with sticky notes. These are recognizable as stock within two seconds and undermine every other claim on the page.

The fix is real photos. An iPhone shot of the actual team in real work clothes beats a polished stock photo every time. We covered the full pattern in [our trust-signals post](/blog/trust-signals-that-move-the-needle/).

### Sin 4: Vague claims

"Passion for excellence." "Customer-centric approach." "Industry-leading service." Phrases like these are the white noise of small business websites. They make no claim that can be verified, so the reader's brain treats them as decoration and skips them.

The fix is replacing each vague claim with a specific one. Not "passion for quality" — "we hand-code every site we ship, and we have shipped over 100." Not "customer-focused" — "you hear back from us within one business day, every time."

## The three-block About page structure

Roughly 500 to 900 words across three blocks, in this order.

### Block 1: The promise (restated)

The first block on the About page is a restatement of who you serve and what they get from you. Not your founding story. Not your team grid. The reader is in diligence mode, and the first thing the page should confirm is that this is the right business for them.

For UI Compass, this block sounds like: "We build hand-coded websites for DFW small businesses. Every site we ship loads in under a second, scores 95+ on mobile PageSpeed, and includes unlimited edits for $150/month."

Three sentences. Restates the homepage promise. Anchors the diligence by reminding the visitor what they came for. Two hundred words maximum.

### Block 2: The proof

The middle block. The heaviest lifting. Real photos, real names, real numbers.

For most small businesses, the proof block contains some mix of:

- **A real photo of the team or the founder.** Working on a real project, in real clothes, at real scale.
- **Named team members with one-line roles.** "Joseph C., founder and developer."
- **One or two numeric proofs.** "Over 100 sites shipped since 2020." "100 PageSpeed score on the homepage you are reading."
- **One paragraph of named testimonials.** Full name, full business, with a photo if possible.
- **Certifications, partner badges, or press logos.** Three to five, real and verifiable.

This is where conversion happens on the About page. A visitor who scrolls past this block without finding something concrete to anchor on usually leaves. A visitor who finds something specific — a real name, a real number, a real photo — is significantly more likely to click through to the contact form.

The same [specific-current-verifiable rules](/blog/trust-signals-that-move-the-needle/) apply on the About page exactly like they apply in the homepage trust block.

### Block 3: The story (filtered)

The last block is the founder origin or company history — but filtered through what it means for the customer.

Wrong: "Joseph founded UI Compass in 2020 after working in agency development since 2015."

Right: "I started UI Compass in 2020 because I watched too many small business owners pay $250 a month for a WordPress site that loaded in six seconds and dropped half their leads. Five years and a hundred sites later, every site we ship loads in under one second, period."

Both contain the founding date. The first is about the founder; the second is about why the founder is relevant to the customer. The visitor reads the second one and thinks, "okay, that's why I should hire this person."

Two hundred to four hundred words. Personal, but pointed.

## What does not belong on the About page

A few items that frequently appear on About pages and almost always belong elsewhere.

### Service descriptions

The About page is not the place to describe each service. The services pages do that. Repeating the service descriptions on the About page is duplicate content, and worse, it dilutes the diligence work the About page should be doing.

### Long company timelines

"Founded in 2020. Hired first employee in 2022. Moved to a new office in 2024." These belong in press kits and acquisition pitches, not on an About page. The visitor does not care about your milestones. They care about whether you will be good for them right now.

### A second contact form

The contact form lives on the contact page. The About page can have a closing CTA pointing at the contact form — but not the form itself. Repeating the form on every page creates the impression that you are over-asking.

## What the rewrite usually costs

For most small business sites, the About-page rewrite is a half-day of focused work. Including:

- Outline the three blocks (30 minutes).
- Write the three blocks (3 hours of actual writing).
- Take or commission real photos to replace stock (separate engagement, but plan for it).
- Implement on the site (30 minutes for a developer, longer on a builder).

The page launches with a measurable conversion lift in our experience — usually 1.5 to 2x on the click-through rate from About to the contact page. Not because the new page is dramatically different in length or structure, but because it is finally answering the right question.
