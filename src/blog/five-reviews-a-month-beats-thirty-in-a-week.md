Five Google reviews a month for six months beats thirty Google reviews in one week.

Most local business owners read that and assume it is wrong. Surely more reviews, faster, is better. It is not. Google's local algorithm has been pattern-matching for review velocity since at least 2018, and a sudden spike trips the same filters that catch fake-review campaigns. The reviews still appear, but they may not count toward your rating, and the listing can be flagged for further review.

The win is the drip, not the flood. This post is the system that gets you the steady drip, the volume threshold that actually matters, and the four shortcuts that get you penalized.

## Why velocity matters more than volume

Google's local search algorithm is built to surface the most relevant business near a searcher. To do that, it has to distinguish "real business with happy customers" from "business gaming the system." Review velocity is one of the strongest signals.

### What "natural" looks like to Google

A real small business doing real work generates reviews at a rate that roughly matches its customer volume. Some weeks more, some weeks fewer. A line plotted over six months trends gently upward. There are no spikes. There are no dead zones longer than a couple of weeks unless the business is seasonal.

A campaign-driven listing looks different. Long flat stretches followed by a vertical jump. Then flat again. Repeat every quarter. Even when the underlying reviews are real, the pattern reads as "asked everyone at once."

### What Google does about the spike

Two filters trigger when a spike crosses Google's threshold. The first is review filtering — some of the reviews simply do not count toward the public rating. The second is listing-level review of the listing itself, which can result in suspension if the pattern repeats.

The conservative rule for owners: aim for a steady cadence and never let weekly volume exceed 2 to 3x your established baseline. If you are averaging 5 reviews a month, you can absorb a busy week of 8. You cannot absorb a week of 25 without consequences.

## The 70% rule on asks

The single highest-leverage stat in this post: about 70% of customers will leave a Google review if you ask. Most owners simply do not ask.

That number sounds high until you do the math against your own customer base. If you serve 40 customers a month and ask all of them, that is 28 potential reviews. Even at the slower realistic conversion rate of half that (because not every ask reaches the customer at the right moment), you are looking at 14 reviews a month — far above the threshold most local businesses ever cross.

The bottleneck is the ask, not the willingness.

## The 40-review threshold

Volume still matters once velocity is healthy. The number that marks the inflection point: roughly 40 reviews.

Listings with under 5 reviews receive a small fraction of the map clicks that listings with 40+ receive — roughly one-third the click volume on the same query. The curve is non-linear: each review under 40 adds noticeably to perceived credibility; each review past 100 adds almost nothing.

Most local businesses we audit sit between 5 and 25 reviews. They are inside the gap. A focused 6-month review-collection program at a steady cadence usually clears 40 by the end and unlocks the click multiplier permanently.

## Six places your direct review link belongs

The fastest way to lift your monthly review count is to make the ask <span class="tooltip-term" data-tooltip="The number of steps or amount of effort between intent and action. Lower friction = higher completion rate. A direct review link removes 3-4 friction steps versus 'search for us on Google.'">low-friction</span>. Replace "leave us a review" with a direct one-click link to the review form.

### 1. Email signature

Every team member's outbound email. One line at the bottom: "Liked working with us? Leave us a quick review →" with the direct link. Adds zero effort per email and reaches every customer touch.

### 2. Invoice or receipt footer

After service, before payment, after payment — pick one and stick with it. The customer is already looking at the document and the moment is fresh.

### 3. Counter or service-vehicle QR code

A printed QR code that opens the review form on the customer's phone. Counter for retail/restaurant. Vehicle decal for trades. The visual reminder is always present without being pushy.

### 4. Back of the business card

Print the QR code on the back of every business card. Most cards live in wallets. The QR is there months later when the customer recommends you to a friend.

### 5. Website footer

A small "Review us on Google" line in the footer of every page. Catches website visitors who already had a good experience and just need the path.

### 6. Thank-you page after a contact-form submission

The customer just opted in. Their attention is already on you. A thank-you page that asks for a review (after the conversion has happened) is among the highest-converting placements.

The same logic about placing the direct link in multiple low-friction spots is what we apply to lead-capture forms — see [the seven-section homepage layout](/blog/the-seven-homepage-sections/) for where the placements go.

## The four shortcuts that get you penalized

Google's policy is unambiguous on these. Each one is enforceable, and each one has been enforced against small businesses we have worked with.

### Shortcut 1: Discounts for reviews

Offering a 10% discount, a free upgrade, or a future credit in exchange for a review. The review gets filtered. The listing accumulates strikes. Repeat offenders lose their star rating.

### Shortcut 2: Contest entries

"Leave a review and be entered to win a prize." Same enforcement, same outcome. The mechanism does not matter — Google's algorithm reads the pattern of incentivized reviews regardless of the specific incentive.

### Shortcut 3: Review gating

<span class="tooltip-term" data-tooltip="Screening customers (often through an internal feedback form) and only directing the happy ones to leave a public review. Explicitly banned by Google's policy.">Review gating</span> means routing happy customers to public review forms while routing unhappy customers to private feedback. The math sounds clever and is explicitly banned. The detection is not subtle either — Google can see the disparity between your survey traffic and your review distribution.

### Shortcut 4: Self-reviews from staff or family

Reviews from your own employees, your spouse, your business partner. Google detects these via IP address, account history, and proximity. The reviews get filtered, the accounts get flagged, and the listing accumulates a strike that future reviews carry forward.

The fix for all four is the same: ask everyone, with no incentive, with no screening, at the right time. Boring. Effective. Within policy.

## A 6-month plan

If you want to clear the 40-review threshold without spiking, a steady-state plan looks like this.

### Month 1: Setup

Generate your direct review link from your Google Business Profile. Place it in the six locations above. Train every team member who has customer contact on the ask script. Decide who tracks the count.

### Months 2–3: Baseline

Start asking every satisfied customer at the natural moment. Track weekly count. Aim for 4 to 6 reviews per month at this stage. The pace is slower than you think it should be — that is fine, that is the point.

### Months 4–5: Steady drip

Maintain the cadence. Every team member asks at the natural moment. Reviews accumulate. The line on the chart trends gently upward. By the end of month 5, you are at 20 to 30 reviews.

### Month 6: The 40-review threshold

The cumulative total crosses 40. Map clicks lift. The 88% trust signal from response rate compounds with the volume threshold. Conversion rate from the listing improves measurably.
