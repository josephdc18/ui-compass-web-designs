A small business homepage is not an art project. It is a salesperson with seven jobs.

The site that converts and the site that does not are usually using the same tools. Same fonts, same color palette, same hosting, same reasonable photographs. The difference is that one of them works through the seven jobs in order and the other one decided three of them were optional.

This post is the order. Use it as a checklist before you write a line of copy or pick a stock photo.

## Why order matters

Visitors do not read your homepage. They scan it. The first scroll decides whether they bother with the second. The second decides whether they bother with the contact form. By the time they have done three scrolls without finding what they came for, they are gone.

Order is the choreography of that scan. A site that puts trust signals before any reason to trust them is begging you to look away. A site that puts the contact form before the reasons to contact is asking too early. The seven sections below run the conversation in the order it actually happens in your visitor's head.

## The seven sections

### Section 1: The hero

The first section <span class="tooltip-term" data-tooltip="The portion of the page visible without scrolling. On a phone, this is roughly the first 600 pixels of vertical space. On a laptop, around 800.">above the fold</span>. It does three things, no more.

- States who the site is for, in plain language.
- Names the outcome they get from working with you.
- Gives one obvious thing to do next (a <span class="tooltip-term" data-tooltip="The most important call-to-action on a page. Usually the contact button or phone number tap. There should only be one per section.">primary CTA</span>, usually a phone number or a quote button).

Headline is the biggest text on the page. Subhead is a single sentence that sells the headline. CTA is one button, not three. If your hero needs a paragraph to explain what the business does, the headline is wrong, not the layout.

The hero is also the section most likely to be the [LCP element on your PageSpeed score](/blog/the-1-second-tax/), so the image you pick here has weight beyond aesthetics.

### Section 2: The proof strip

The second screen, immediately below the hero. The shortest section on the page.

A row of logos, a row of star ratings, a row of certifications, or one strong testimonial pulled out and oversized. Pick whichever you actually have. The job of this section is to keep the visitor on the page after the hero by giving them one reason to believe the headline is not lying.

Sites that skip this section lose visitors at the seam between the hero and the services list. The proof strip is the bridge. It is also a signal in [our redesign-or-optimize scoreboard](/blog/redesign-or-optimize-warning-signs/) — sites without it tend to score multiple warning signs at once.

### Section 3: The services or offerings

The third section. The most-skimmed area of any small business homepage.

This is a card grid (three to four cards, almost always) summarizing what you actually do. Each card has a name, a one-line description, and a link to the full service page. No more. The temptation is to write paragraphs here. Resist.

If your business has more than four services, pick the four that bring in 80% of the revenue and put the rest behind a "see all services" link. The homepage is not your full inventory. It is the map. For UI Compass, the four cards on our homepage map to [web design](/web-design/), [web development](/web-development/), [unlimited edits and support](/unlimited-edits-and-support/), and our redesign-every-three-years cadence.

### Section 4: The differentiator

The fourth section. The hardest one to write.

This is where you answer the implicit question every visitor is asking by now: "Why you, instead of any of the ten other people who showed up in the same Google result?" One sentence. Then three to five sub-points that prove the sentence.

Generic differentiators ("we care about quality") are worse than no differentiator at all, because they signal that you do not have one. Specific differentiators sound like, "every site we ship is hand-coded, scores 95+ on PageSpeed, and gets a free redesign every three years." Three concrete claims that competitors cannot trivially copy. That is the bar. Our [DFW comparison post](/blog/comparing-local-dfw-web-design-companies/) walks through how to spot a differentiator that earns the right to be on the page.

### Section 5: The work

The fifth section. Your portfolio, your case studies, your before-and-afters, your gallery.

Pick the format that matches your work. Tradespeople, restaurants, photographers, and contractors should use a visual gallery. Service businesses, agencies, and consultancies should use case-study cards with one outcome metric per card. Either way, the format is "show, do not tell."

Sites without a work section are betting that the visitor will trust the differentiator on faith. Most will not.

### Section 6: The trust block

The sixth section. Testimonials, reviews, awards, press, partner logos, an "as seen in" strip.

This is where most small business sites under-invest. Two testimonials buried at the bottom is not enough. You want at least four, with real names and either real photos or real businesses attached. Pull-quote one of them. Leave the rest as cards.

Testimonials are also indexable content. Real customer language ranks for queries that your "About Us" page never will, because real customers describe their problems in the words their peers are searching for. The trust block is doing two jobs at once. If you mark up the testimonials with [Review schema](/blog/faq-schema-3x-screen-space/), they show up under your search listing too.

### Section 7: The closing CTA

The seventh section. The job that the rest of the page exists to set up.

This is not a link buried in the footer. It is a section, with its own headline ("Ready to talk?"), its own visual weight, and its own CTA. Often the same CTA as the hero, but second time. If the visitor has gotten this far, they are interested enough that asking is no longer pushy. Asking is the favor.

Pair the closing CTA with secondary contact options (phone number, email, calendar link) for visitors who do not want to fill out a form. The primary CTA is the lead-gen one. The secondary ones are the bypass. We point ours at [the contact page](/contact/) and a calendar link.

## What about an "About" section?

You will notice "About" is not in the list. That is intentional.

Most "About" sections on small business homepages are written in the third person, lead with the founder's biography, and never quite get to the point of why the visitor should care. If you have a strong founder story that sells the business (rare but real), it goes in the differentiator section as a sub-point, not as its own section. The full story belongs on a dedicated [About page](/about/), linked from the navigation.

The exception is local service businesses where the visitor is hiring a person, not a company. There, a short "meet the team" strip can replace or extend the trust block. But it is still not a separate stand-alone section, and it does not go before the services.

## What spacing actually does

The other half of the homepage equation is white space.

### Section padding rules

A reasonable section padding on mobile is around 50 pixels top and bottom. On desktop, 75 to 100. Less than that and the sections run into each other. More than that and the visitor wonders if they have reached the bottom of the page.

### Type-scale rules

Body text reads best at a <span class="tooltip-term" data-tooltip="The vertical space between lines of text, measured as a multiple of the font size. 1.5 means each line is 1.5x the font height — comfortable reading rhythm.">line-height</span> of 1.4 to 1.5. Headings are at least 1.5 times the body font size, usually closer to two times. These are not aesthetic preferences. They are how the eye scans without fatigue. Builders that ship with tighter line heights "to save space" are saving space against the visitor's reading experience.

### Why white space is not empty space

White space is not empty space. It is the breathing room that makes everything else readable. The same logic governs our [mobile-first layouts](/blog/designed-on-a-monitor-used-on-a-phone/) — the 320-pixel layout has the most generous white space proportionally because the screen has the least room to give.

## Where we land

We use this seven-section pattern as the default for every homepage we ship. Not because the pattern is creative, but because it is reliable. Once we know the order works, the design conversation moves up a level: what makes this hero say something different than the next agency's, what proof points actually exist for this business, what work is worth showing.

If your current homepage is missing one of the seven, that is the section to add. If it has all seven but in the wrong order, that is the rearrangement to do before any other change. Either way, [our pricing](/pricing/) folds the homepage rebuild into the same flat $150/month as everything else.
