Web Development Done Right

Development is what separates a good website from an exceptional one. There is a great deal of misinformation about how websites are built and how they should perform. Suggestions that code is no longer relevant are simply incorrect.

Right-click any website and select "Inspect" — you will see thousands of lines of code. Every website on the internet relies on it. The question is not whether code is involved, but how well it is written.

You have several choices when commissioning a website. Drag-and-drop platforms such as Wix or Squarespace are convenient, but they compromise on code quality, producing slower sites that search engines find harder to interpret. WordPress can be highly effective with sufficient time and budget, but without those resources, projects often rely on plugins that introduce performance issues and security vulnerabilities.

We have seen businesses lose thousands of dollars in revenue while attempting to save a few hundred on their website.

That is why our process is structured around delivering quality from the outset.

Why Speed Matters

Our development practices are oriented around one core objective: delivering a website that loads quickly. A website is a collection of files served to a visitor over the internet. The smaller and more efficient those files are, the faster the experience. Most of our pages are 2,000–3,000 lines of code, compared with 12,000 or more lines on competing platforms. Every line we eliminate translates into measurably faster load times. We also resize, compress, and optimize every image and icon, and host the result on a global network so that content is delivered from a server closest to your visitor.

The less code that needs to load, the faster the experience. Each kilobyte saved is another fraction of a second your customer is not waiting. A two-second delay in load time can result in an abandonment rate of up to 87%. Visitor patience continues to decline, and every advantage matters.

Exceptional Website Security

Many websites have weak security postures. A website is attacked every 39 seconds. Most sites are built with databases, plugins, and multiple layers of code, each of which represents a potential vulnerability.

Every plugin is a potential entry point, every database connection is a possible exploit, and every routine update can introduce risk. The majority of attacks are automated: bots continuously scan the internet for sites running known plugins or technologies that have unpatched issues. For most small businesses, a backend is unnecessary — a well-organized site of text and images, presented through clear design, is sufficient. All of that can be delivered through a static website.

A static website has no database to compromise, no plugins to exploit, and no admin panel exposed to attackers. The result is a more secure platform that demands less ongoing attention from you.

Website Accessibility

More than 61 million adults in the United States live with a disability, many of whom encounter difficulty accessing online content. With only 5.2% of the internet currently meeting accessibility standards, an accessible website is both an ethical and legal priority.

Legal Implications of an Inaccessible Website

An inaccessible website may expose your business to legal risk. Accessibility legislation continues to expand worldwide:

  • United States: The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) has been the basis for thousands of lawsuits against businesses with inaccessible websites. Legal fees from these cases routinely exceed the cost of building an accessible site from the start.
  • European Union: Any business serving EU customers must comply with the European Accessibility Act, including conformance to WCAG 2.1 standards.
  • United Kingdom: The Equality Act of 2010 requires websites and mobile apps to be perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust, with particular emphasis on public-sector entities.
  • Australia: The Disability Discrimination Act 1992 requires public-facing websites to be accessible.
  • Argentina: Law 26,653 requires both public and private websites to meet accessibility standards.

How to Make Your Website Accessible

  • Add alternative text to images so that screen readers can convey content to visitors who are blind or partially sighted.
  • Add ARIA labels to links and buttons without text content (a cart icon, for example, is meaningless to a screen reader without a label).
  • Ensure that color combinations provide sufficient contrast for users with color blindness.
  • Use semantic heading levels (H1, H2, H3, etc.) to allow screen readers to interpret the page hierarchy correctly.
  • Maintain a logical tab order that follows the structure of the menu, so the site can be navigated using only the keyboard.
  • Include breadcrumb navigation that reflects the user's location within the site.
  • For text that uses styling (bold, color) to convey emphasis, ensure that the meaning is also clear to assistive technologies.
