You have three quotes on your desk. They use different words for the same things. One is $3,000 up front. One is $189 a month. One refuses to put a number on it until the second meeting.

This guide is a framework for cutting through that. Five questions to ask any local web designer in the Dallas-Fort Worth metroplex. Then a quick walk through three Arlington-area shops you might shortlist, including ours.

The goal is not to crown a winner. The goal is to give you the same vocabulary every shop is using internally so you can spot a red flag in a meeting.

## Why local still matters in DFW

You can hire a designer in any time zone. Plenty of agencies will quote you happily from Mumbai or Miami. So why does local matter.

Three reasons.

1. **Same-day site visits.** Photographing your shop, your team, and your trucks beats stock photography every time. A local shop can drive over.
2. **Same time zone, same business hours.** When your form stops delivering on a Tuesday at 4pm, you do not want to file a ticket and wait for India to wake up.
3. **A handshake context.** DFW is a referral economy. Your designer's other clients are probably people you already know. Reputation has a shorter feedback loop here than it does anywhere else.

Local is not a tiebreaker. It is a structural advantage. Treat it as one.

## The five questions that actually matter

Most "how to choose a web designer" articles list ten or twenty questions. Most are filler. These five separate the shops that build for you from the shops that build for themselves.

### 1. What is the pricing model, and what is included for life

You are looking for the difference between **one-time** and **monthly**.

- **One-time** means you pay a lump sum up front. The site is yours. So is the maintenance bill. Hosting, plugin licenses, security patches, and edits become line items the day after launch.
- **Monthly** means you pay a recurring fee that covers some bundle of build, hosting, edits, and ongoing support. The number is smaller per month but it does not stop.

Neither is wrong. They suit different cash-flow situations. What you want is a shop that tells you which they are without you having to ask three times. If post-launch support is sold as a separate "care plan" you have to opt into, that is one-time pricing dressed up. If "unlimited edits" stops at five hours a month, that is hourly billing dressed up.

### 2. What technology will your site be built on

The honest answers are: hand-coded, page builder (Wix, Squarespace, Webflow, Shopify), or WordPress with a theme.

Each has consequences.

- **Hand-coded** sites are the fastest. They have the smallest attack surface. They cost the most to build. They cost the least to keep alive over five years.
- **Page builder** sites are the cheapest to build. They are the slowest at scale. You are renting your site from a platform that can change pricing or shutter features any quarter.
- **WordPress** sits in the middle. Cheap to start. Plugin debt accumulates. Page speed caps lower than hand-coded.

If a designer cannot tell you which of those three categories your site falls into in one sentence, that is a red flag. They are about to build you whatever is fastest for them.

### 3. What PageSpeed score will you deliver, and on what device

The relevant number is **mobile PageSpeed Insights**. Desktop scores are pretty for marketing screenshots. Mobile is where 60% of your traffic actually lands.

A site under a 90 mobile score in 2026 is leaving leads on the table. A site under 70 is bleeding. The shops that quote you a number, in writing, with a "minimum mobile score we will hand off" clause, are the shops that have actually optimized a site before. The shops that say "fast enough" have not.

### 4. What does ongoing support look like, in writing

Sites do not break on Tuesdays at 10am. They break Saturday at 11pm, on a holiday, the day before your big sale.

You want answers to:

- Who picks up the phone after hours
- What is the response-time SLA in hours
- Is there a per-request fee on top of the monthly retainer
- Who owns the domain, the hosting account, the analytics, the email, the CMS login

Get it in writing. The shops that resist putting any of this in writing are the shops that will resist your fourth email next April.

### 5. Will you still be in business in three years

Your site is a three-year asset. The shop building it has to last at least that long.

Look at:

- How long they have been operating (years, not "since 2024")
- How many active clients they list publicly
- Whether they have one developer or a team, and whether the answer matters to you
- Whether they are a side hustle for someone with a day job, or their full-time business

There is no right answer. A solo full-time builder can be more reliable than a venture-funded agency. But you should know which you are buying.

## A side-by-side checklist for your shortlist

Bring this to every discovery call. Two minutes of clarity per question.

- Pricing model is named (one-time, monthly, or hybrid) and the included list is in writing
- Build technology is named (hand-coded, page builder, WordPress)
- Minimum delivered mobile PageSpeed score is committed to in writing
- After-hours support, response SLA, and any per-request fees are in writing
- Domain, hosting, email, analytics, and CMS ownership are spelled out
- You have seen a live client site at least three years old
- You have spoken to at least one current client without the designer in the room

The shop that comes back with all seven without flinching is the shop you should hire. The rest are auditioning, not selling.

## Five questions to take into the meeting

Cut these out and bring them.

1. "Walk me through your last launch, scored on mobile PageSpeed Insights right now."
2. "What is the cheapest add-on past the base contract, and what is the most expensive."
3. "What happens to my site, my domain, and my email if you stop responding to email for thirty days."
4. "Who owns the code when our contract ends."
5. "What is one thing you would do differently if you were rebuilding your own site today."

The fifth one is the tell. A shop that has nothing to say to that question has not thought hard enough about their own work.
