Squarespace is $200 a year. Wix is similar. Shopify is more.

We are $1,800 a year, managed.

The eight-times difference is real and the comparison gets asked at every sales call. The honest version of the answer is below, with the trade-offs both ways.

## What the $200 buys

Squarespace, Wix, and Shopify all sell the same basic product. You pay the platform a monthly fee. You get a drag-and-drop editor. You pick a template. You swap in your content. The platform hosts the site. You are the developer, the designer, the writer, and the IT department.

For that fee you get:
- The editor.
- The hosting.
- A basic SSL certificate.
- A free domain for the first year (then $20–$30/yr after).
- A handful of integrations (Stripe, MailChimp, Calendly).
- A theme that looks like a Squarespace site.

That is a real product. It works. Hundreds of thousands of small businesses use it. We have never argued otherwise.

## What the $200 does not buy

Three things that matter for most small businesses.

**Speed under one second on mobile.** Page-builder sites typically land between PageSpeed 50 and 70 on mobile. The platform is built for editor convenience, not for raw delivery speed. The visitor pays the cost on every page load. <a href="/blog/the-1-second-tax/">A one-second delay drops conversions about 7 percent.</a> Most builder sites are 3–6 seconds slower than they need to be.

**Competitive local SEO.** Builder platforms include "SEO settings" panels that handle page titles and meta descriptions. They do not handle the deeper structural work: NAP consistency, location schema, service-area mapping, internal linking strategy, content velocity. For low-competition niches it is enough. For competitive ones (plumber, attorney, photographer, contractor in any major metro) it is not.

**ADA accessibility.** Most builder templates fail basic WCAG 2.2 AA contrast and form-label requirements. <a href="/blog/wcag-2-2-aa-in-five-minutes/">Most owners do not realize this until a demand letter arrives.</a> Settlements run $5K to $25K. Fixing the templates retroactively is harder than building accessible from the start.

## The hidden cost: your time

This is the one nobody factors in correctly.

A passable Squarespace site takes most small business owners 25 to 40 hours to build. That includes:
- Picking a template.
- Writing the copy for every page.
- Finding or buying images.
- Setting up the contact form and testing it.
- Wrestling with the editor when it refuses to do the thing you want.
- Fixing the mobile breakpoints that look wrong.
- Setting up analytics.
- Re-doing sections after you realize the copy was vague.

Plus, after launch, the recurring tax:
- An hour a month responding to "the form is broken" or "the photo is the wrong size."
- A weekend every quarter when you update content.
- A full day every year for the platform update that breaks two things.

At $50 an hour (a low estimate of small-business-owner time value), 40 launch hours + 30 ongoing hours per year = $3,500 in year one labor. After year one, $1,500/yr in ongoing time.

The Squarespace fee was $200. The actual cost was closer to $3,700 in year one.

## What the $1,800 buys

The managed alternative looks like this:

- A hand-coded site, no page builder, no WordPress. Custom code for your business, not a template.
- Hosting on an edge network with sub-1-second mobile load times.
- PageSpeed scores in the 90s. Often 100.
- Real local SEO: schema markup, NAP consistency, service-area mapping, internal linking.
- Accessibility compliance from day one. WCAG 2.2 AA passing.
- Unlimited edits. You email us what you want changed. We change it. No DIY.
- Security patching, uptime monitoring, broken-link cleanup, all silent.
- A free redesign every three years so the site never gets dated.

Your time cost: a few minutes per email asking for an edit. Total in year one: maybe 5 hours. Maybe.

At $50/hr, that is $250 in your labor. Plus $1,800 in our fee. Total cost of ownership: $2,050.

DIY cost in year one: $3,700.
Managed cost in year one: $2,050.

Managed wins on total cost. In year two it widens further (DIY ongoing tax stays, managed stays at $1,800).

## When DIY is genuinely the right call

Four cases:

1. **Pre-revenue.** No customers, no income, no urgency. Start with a $10 domain and a free landing page. Upgrade later.
2. **You enjoy the platform.** Some owners like building. The site is a hobby project. Fine. Just count the time honestly.
3. **One product, one page.** A weekend launch for a single offer. Carrd or a single Squarespace page is genuinely the right tool.
4. **You need it live in 48 hours.** No agency can ship that fast. DIY can.

If you are in one of those four buckets, DIY is the right call. If you are not, the math usually says hire it out.

## The "I will just learn it" trap

The most expensive version of this decision is the one where the owner says "I will just learn Squarespace, it cannot be that hard."

It is not hard. It is time-consuming. And the time keeps coming. The platform updates. The plugin you used last year is deprecated. The form integration breaks. The image suddenly displays at the wrong size. Each fix is small. The cumulative drain is large.

Owners who have done this for two years can usually quote you the date they wished they had hired it out.

## How to decide

Two questions:

**Are you in one of the four "DIY is genuinely right" buckets?** If yes, DIY.

**Do you value your time at more than $50 an hour?** If yes, managed beats DIY on year-one math. If no, the comparison is closer and you can run it on the platform.

For most small businesses in the DFW metroplex, the answer to the second question is yes, even if the owner does not bill themselves at that rate. The labor that goes into the website is labor that did not go into customer work, sales calls, or rest. The opportunity cost is real.
