A pretty website that nobody finds is an expensive business card.

The single most common failure mode for small business websites is that the owner pours weeks into how it looks and zero weeks into whether it actually does the four things a website is supposed to do. The site goes live. The owner shows it to friends. The friends say "looks great." Six months later there have been no leads from the site and nobody knows why.

The site fails for the same reason most do. A working small business website has four properties. Drop any one and the whole thing collapses into decoration. Here they are, in the order to fix them.

## 1. Findable

The website has to show up when people search for what you sell.

This is the property most often skipped because the owner does not see it. The site looks great in the browser. The owner does not realize that for everyone else, the site does not exist until Google decides to show it.

Findable means three things at the foundation level. Your business name appears on every page. Your service plus your city is in your headline and your page titles. Your real address is in the footer. Skip those and Google does not know what your site is about, where you are, or what you do.

Past the foundation, findable means publishing things. A blog post for each service you sell, written for the search terms a customer would actually type. Three months of consistent content and a small business in DFW will start ranking for at least one service-plus-city search. Six months and it will rank for three or four.

The cost of skipping this property is the leads you never see. They are invisible. They search, they do not find you, they find someone else. You never know.

## 2. Believable

In the first 0.05 seconds, a visitor decides whether the site looks like a real business. That is not a typo. Five hundredths of a second. Faster than a blink.

In that window the visitor is grading four things. Your photo (real or stock). Your address (specific or vague). Your reviews (named with dates or anonymous). Your design (clean and current or 2010 stock template).

You only need to win all four. A real photo of you or your shop, taken on a real camera, in the same lighting your actual customers see. A real street address with a city and zip code. Three or more reviews with first and last names and recent dates. A clean design that does not look like a free template from a decade ago.

Stock photos of generic professionals fail this test instantly. "Trusted by industry leaders" with no logos fails this test. "5-star ratings" with no source fails this test. The visitor is not consciously evaluating any of this. They are forming an impression. The impression is either "real business" or "skip."

Half of prospects who land on a site that fails the believability test will not pursue you further. They will not say no. They will just not say yes. The site does the rejecting for you.

## 3. Fast

Past three seconds of load time, you lose 53 percent of mobile visitors. Past six seconds, you lose 79 percent. These are not numbers we invented. They are published industry research, replicated across thousands of sites.

Most builder sites in the wild load between four and eight seconds on mobile. That is the silent killer. The visitor arrives, the page hangs, they swipe back to Google before the page renders. The visit does not count. The bounce does not show up in your form submissions because there was no submission. There was no visit.

Fast is one second or less on mobile. That requires two things working together. Premium hosting on an edge network (so the server starts responding in under 100ms) and code that is not bloated with framework overhead. <a href="/blog/the-10x-load-time-gap/">Hand-coded sites run about 10x faster than page-builder sites at the same content level.</a> The gap is not configurable.

The cost of being slow is the same as the cost of not being findable. The visit happens, the bounce happens, you never see it.

## 4. Convertible

A working website has exactly one visible call to action above the fold on mobile. One. Not three.

The brain processes the homepage in three to five seconds. It can hold one decision in that window. "Call now." "Get a free quote." "Schedule a consultation." Pick one. Make it visible without scrolling. Make it the same one on every page.

Three competing CTAs is a confused page. The visitor's brain interprets confusion as "skip this one." A clear single action gets the click. A vague pile of options gets the back button.

Past the primary CTA, every page should have a secondary path. A way to call you on mobile (tap to dial). A way to email you (real address, not "contact us through the form"). A way to see your reviews. But the primary CTA is the headline of the page. Everything else supports it.

## What this looks like in practice

For a typical service business in DFW, the four properties working together look like this.

A homepage with the headline "[Your service] in [your city]." A photo of the owner. A "Call Now" button visible above the fold on a phone. Three named reviews with dates. A service-area map. A footer with the real address, the licensing number, and the year founded.

Loading in under a second.

Ranking on the first page for "[your service] [your city]" within ninety days.

A contact form submission every five to fifteen days, depending on the size of the market and the competition.

That is the working version. Anything missing from the four properties shows up as a missing lead.

## How to grade your current site

Three numbers:

- Open Google Search Console. Look at impressions for the last 30 days. Under 1,000 means findable is failing.
- Open pagespeed.web.dev and run your homepage on mobile. Under 70 means fast is failing.
- Count the contact form submissions you got last month. Zero means convertible is failing (or you are not getting traffic, see number 1).

Believability is the hardest to self-grade. Show your homepage to three people who do not know your business and ask them, in the first three seconds, "is this a real company?" If two of them hesitate, believable is failing.

The fix in each case is different. The diagnosis is the same. The site is not doing its job because one of the four properties is broken.
