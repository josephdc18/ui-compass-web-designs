The Dallas-Fort Worth web design market is not one market. It is three.

The first tier is small-business and boutique work: lead-generation sites for service businesses, brochure sites for local trades, simple e-commerce for owner-operated retail. The second tier is growth-stage companies that want web design tied tightly to ongoing SEO, content, and digital marketing. The third tier is premium and enterprise work: B2B repositioning, complex commerce, headless and product-marketing sites for funded software companies.

Almost every "wrong fit" hire we see is a buyer who shopped across tiers without realizing they were three different markets. A founder in the third tier interviewing a first-tier shop will conclude the first-tier shop is "too small." A small business in the first tier asking a third-tier agency for a quote will get a number that has an extra zero. Both interpretations are wrong. The shops are not better or worse. They serve different buyers.

This post is the directory. Twelve DFW agencies, segmented by tier, with addresses, services, public pricing where it exists, and an honest read on who each is for. The data is current as of May 2026 and was pulled from each firm's public-facing site, with LinkedIn and Clutch as secondary verification.

## How to evaluate any of these shops

Before we walk through each firm, the framework. These five questions are how a buyer in any tier should pressure-test any DFW shop on their shortlist, ours included.

### 1. What is the pricing model, and what is included for life

The two honest answers are **one-time** and **monthly**.

- **One-time** means a lump sum up front. The site is yours. So is every maintenance bill after launch.
- **Monthly** means a recurring fee that bundles build, hosting, edits, and support. Smaller per month, never stops.

Neither is wrong. Both suit different cash-flow situations. What you want is a shop that names which they are without you asking three times. If post-launch support is sold as a separate "care plan" you have to opt into, that is one-time pricing dressed up. If "unlimited edits" stops at five hours a month, that is hourly billing dressed up.

### 2. What technology will your site be built on

The honest answers are: hand-coded, page builder (Wix, Squarespace, Webflow), or WordPress with a theme.

Hand-coded sites are the fastest, have the smallest attack surface, and cost the most up front but the least to maintain. Page builders are cheapest to start, slowest at scale, and rent-dependent. WordPress sits in the middle and accumulates plugin debt the longer you keep it running.

If a designer cannot tell you which of those three categories your site falls into in one sentence, that is a red flag. They are about to build you whatever is fastest for them. The full speed math is in [The 10x Load-Time Gap](/blog/the-10x-load-time-gap/).

### 3. What PageSpeed score will you deliver, and on what device

The relevant number is **mobile [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/)**. Desktop scores are pretty for marketing screenshots. Mobile is where 60% of your traffic lands.

A site under a 90 mobile score in 2026 is leaving leads on the table. A site under 70 is bleeding. The shops that commit a minimum mobile score in writing have actually optimized a site before. The shops that say "fast enough" have not. Why each second of load time costs roughly 7% of conversions is covered in [The 1-Second Tax](/blog/the-1-second-tax/).

### 4. What does ongoing support look like, in writing

Sites do not break on Tuesdays at 10am. They break Saturday at 11pm, on a holiday, the day before your big sale.

Get answers, in writing, to: who picks up after hours, what the response SLA is in hours, whether there is a per-request fee on top of the monthly retainer, and who owns the domain, hosting, analytics, email, and CMS login. Shops that resist putting this in writing will resist your fourth email next April.

### 5. Will you still be in business in three years

A website is a three-year asset. The shop building it should last at least that long.

Look at how long they have operated (years, not "since 2024"), how many active clients they list publicly, whether they have one developer or a team, and whether it is their full-time business or a side hustle. There is no right answer. A solo full-time builder can be more reliable than a venture-funded agency. You should know which you are buying.

## A side-by-side checklist for your shortlist

Bring this to every discovery call. Two minutes of clarity per question.

- Pricing model is named (one-time, monthly, or hybrid) and the included list is in writing
- Build technology is named (hand-coded, page builder, WordPress)
- Minimum delivered mobile PageSpeed score is committed to in writing
- After-hours support, response SLA, and any per-request fees are in writing
- Domain, hosting, email, analytics, and CMS ownership are spelled out
- You have seen a live client site at least three years old
- You have spoken to at least one current client without the designer in the room

The shop that comes back with all seven without flinching is the shop you should hire. The rest are auditioning, not selling.

## Five questions to take into the meeting

Cut these out and bring them.

1. "Walk me through your last launch, scored on mobile [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/) right now."
2. "What is the cheapest add-on past the base contract, and what is the most expensive."
3. "What happens to my site, my domain, and my email if you stop responding to email for thirty days."
4. "Who owns the code when our contract ends."
5. "What is one thing you would do differently if you were rebuilding your own site today."

The fifth one is the tell. A shop that has nothing to say to that question has not thought hard enough about their own work.

On reviewed public pages, the clearest unresolved legal-name gaps were UI Compass (us), Icepick, Big D Creative, JSL Marketing & Web Design, and Thrive Internet Marketing Agency. We treated those conservatively and flagged them rather than overstating certainty.

Pricing bands move quarterly on Clutch. Team sizes shift more often than firms update their LinkedIn pages. Office addresses and founding dates are the most stable fields and were verified against each firm's own contact page.
