Multiple small businesses have been sued for ADA web violations after installing an accessibility overlay widget.

The widget was supposed to be the fix. The marketing copy on every overlay vendor's site promises exactly that — paste in one line of JavaScript, instant WCAG conformance, lawsuit shield activated. The plaintiffs and the courts have not agreed. The [National Federation of the Blind](https://www.nfb.org/) has been on record against overlay widgets since 2021. Lawsuits have continued. In some cases, the overlay itself has been cited as evidence that the business knew there was a problem and chose to paper over it.

This post is why overlays do not work, the four ways they actively make accessibility worse, and what to ship instead.

## What an <span class="tooltip-term" data-tooltip="A JavaScript widget that loads on every page and attempts to fix accessibility problems at runtime by inserting ARIA attributes, swapping colors, and offering a settings toolbar. Does not satisfy WCAG or the ADA.">overlay widget</span> actually does

Every overlay product follows roughly the same architecture. The vendor gives you a snippet of JavaScript, you paste it into your site's `<head>`, and the widget loads on every page.

Once loaded, the widget does four things:

- **Injects <span class="tooltip-term" data-tooltip="Accessible Rich Internet Applications. A set of HTML attributes (role, aria-label, etc.) that provide accessibility information to assistive technology when native HTML semantics are insufficient.">ARIA</span> attributes** onto elements it thinks need them. A `<div>` styled like a button gets `role="button"` added at runtime.
- **Renders a settings toolbar** in the corner of the page, offering controls for contrast, font size, link highlighting, and a handful of other adjustments.
- **Generates alt text** for images that lack it, using an automated image-recognition service.
- **Attempts contrast fixes** by overlaying CSS rules on top of your existing styles.

Each of these is a patch on top of the real problem. None of them resolve the real problem. And several of them introduce problems that were not there before.

## Why overlays do not satisfy the law

WCAG conformance is a property of your site's rendered HTML and CSS. Tools that evaluate accessibility — the <span class="tooltip-term" data-tooltip="Web Accessibility Evaluation Tool. A free scanner at wave.webaim.org that flags accessibility issues on any page.">WAVE</span> scanner, axe-core, Lighthouse, manual screen-reader testing — read the document the browser actually renders. The overlay sits on top of that document. The underlying HTML still has 40 contrast failures, the headings are still out of order, the images still have no alt text in the source.

Courts have not been persuaded that an overlay constitutes a "good faith effort" toward compliance. Several cases have specifically referenced overlay installation in the complaint, arguing that the business is aware of the accessibility problem (otherwise why install the widget?) but chose a known-ineffective shortcut.

The legal bar is reasonable accessibility of the actual site to the actual user. Overlays do not provide that. They provide a settings toolbar in the corner.

## The four ways overlays actively make accessibility worse

### 1. Focus traps

Overlay widgets render their toolbar as a modal or popup. Once a keyboard user tabs into the toolbar, the focus often does not return to the page. The visitor gets stuck navigating the toolbar's options forever.

This is a textbook WCAG violation (Success Criterion 2.1.2: No Keyboard Trap). It is also a real-world experience problem for the exact users the widget claims to help.

### 2. Conflicting ARIA

The widget injects ARIA attributes onto elements that may already have proper ones. Or it injects attributes onto elements where they actively confuse screen readers (a `role="button"` on something that is already a `<button>`).

Conflicting ARIA is a known anti-pattern. Screen readers parse the ARIA tree to decide what to announce; when two sources disagree about what an element is, the announcement becomes unpredictable. The visitor gets less information, not more.

### 3. Performance damage

The overlay is a 50 to 200KB JavaScript bundle that runs on every page load. It typically blocks rendering for 100 to 400 milliseconds while it scans the DOM and inserts its patches.

That is real load-time cost. On a site already struggling with [the 1-second tax](/blog/the-1-second-tax/), the overlay can push your mobile PageSpeed score down 5 to 15 points. The visitor with low vision, or the visitor on a slow connection — the same people the widget claims to help — pays this cost first.

### 4. Fragile fixes that break on the next site update

The overlay's patches assume the existing HTML structure. When you update your site (a new plugin, a redesigned page, a content change), the overlay's selectors may no longer match. The patches it relied on yesterday silently stop working today, and nobody notices until a customer complains or a demand letter arrives.

Real accessibility lives in the underlying code, where it survives content changes. Overlay patches live in the air above the code, where they snap on the next gust.

## What real accessibility looks like instead

The work is not glamorous, and it is not one line of JavaScript. The work is the four-fix pattern we covered in [our WCAG 2.2 AA in 5 minutes post](/blog/wcag-2-2-aa-in-five-minutes/):

- **Real contrast** in the CSS, not patched at runtime.
- **Visible focus indicators** that survive every site update.
- **Real alt text** written by the person who knows what the image shows.
- **Semantic HTML**: `<button>` for buttons, `<a href>` for links, `<ul>` for lists, `<h1>` through `<h6>` in order.

Add in keyboard-tested navigation, form labels that actually label, and skip links for screen-reader users. None of this is exotic engineering. It is just the default-correct thing instead of the default-pretty thing.

### What the work pays back

Sites that ship accessible-by-default typically score better on:

- **SEO** — semantic HTML, proper headings, descriptive alt text all feed Google's local and AI search pipelines (per [our AI-crawlers post](/blog/unblock-ai-crawlers/)).
- **PageSpeed** — no overlay bundle to load, no patches to apply at runtime.
- **Conversion** — readable contrast, generous tap targets, and visible focus signals all help every visitor, not just the ones with declared disabilities.

The work that satisfies an accessibility audit is mostly the same work that makes a site easier to use for everybody.

## What to do if you have an overlay installed today

Three steps, in order.

### Step 1: Remove the overlay

Pull the JavaScript snippet out of your site's `<head>`. Cancel the subscription. You are no longer paying $49 to $490 a month for a patch that did not work.

### Step 2: Run the three tests from our WCAG 2.2 AA post

[Open WAVE](https://wave.webaim.org/). Run the keyboard navigation test. Audit your alt text. The three tests from [our 5-minute post](/blog/wcag-2-2-aa-in-five-minutes/) tell you how big the actual gap is.

### Step 3: Ship the four fixes

Real contrast, visible focus, real alt text, semantic HTML. Depending on the starting state, that is 4 to 12 hours of focused work on a typical small business site. Most of the work is one-time. The maintenance after launch is a quarterly pass.
