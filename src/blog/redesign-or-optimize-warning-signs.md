You don't need a redesign. You need a diagnostic.

Half the time someone calls us asking for a rebuild, the actual fix is a 90-minute optimization pass. The other half, they have been propping up a site that should have been rebuilt eighteen months ago. The difference is not taste. It is how many warning signs the site is showing right now.

Count them with the scoreboard below. The number you land on tells you whether you should be reaching for a credit card, a punch list, or neither.

## The seven warning signs

Each of these is binary. Either the sign is showing or it is not. Do not grade on a curve.

### Sign 1: The mobile experience is broken

Open your site on the phone you actually carry. Tap a button. Scroll a section. Submit your contact form.

If you can read the site, navigate it, and complete a primary action without zooming or rage-tapping, you pass. If anything makes you wince, you fail. Half of small business sites fail this test on the homepage alone. The deeper read on this is in our post on [why mobile-first matters](/blog/designed-on-a-monitor-used-on-a-phone/).

### Sign 2: The site loads slower than three seconds

Run your homepage through Google's PageSpeed Insights. Look at the <span class="tooltip-term" data-tooltip="Largest Contentful Paint — how long until the biggest visible thing on the page renders.">LCP</span> number on mobile. If it is over 2.5 seconds, you are past the threshold where Google starts counting it against you. If it is over 4 seconds, you are losing a measurable chunk of every visitor before they read a word.

The full math on what each second costs you is in [The 7% Conversion Tax of a 1-Second Delay](/blog/the-1-second-tax/). If your homepage scores under 80 on mobile, this sign is showing.

### Sign 3: The visual design is dated

This one is uncomfortable but specific. Pull up three of your top five competitors. Set your homepage next to theirs in three browser tabs. Switch through them quickly.

If your site reads as the older one, you have a hierarchy problem (or a font problem, or a stock-photo problem). If your site reads as the modern one, this sign is not showing. For what a current homepage actually looks like, see our breakdown of [the seven sections every small business homepage needs](/blog/the-seven-homepage-sections/).

### Sign 4: The conversion path is leaky

A <span class="tooltip-term" data-tooltip="The chain of pages and clicks that turns a visitor into a lead. Usually homepage → service page → contact form on a small business site.">conversion path</span> is everything between "visitor lands on the page" and "we have their information." On a small business site, that is usually homepage, then service page, then contact form. Or homepage, then a tap on the phone number.

If you cannot say what your conversion path is in one sentence, that is the sign. If you can say it but you are not sure how often it works, run a single test. Send three friends to the site and ask them to "ask for a quote." Watch what they do.

### Sign 5: Traffic has declined for six straight months

Open Google Analytics. Pull twelve months of organic search traffic. Look for a sustained downward trend, not a seasonal dip.

Six months of decline is the threshold. One bad month is noise. Three is a flag. Six is a problem your homepage cannot ignore. If the decline started after a Google update or a robots.txt change, the cause might be technical rather than structural — see our walk-through of [robots.txt and the AI crawlers](/blog/unblock-ai-crawlers/) for one common cause.

### Sign 6: Something is technically broken

Browser console errors. Forms that say "submitted" but never deliver. Images that render as broken-link icons on iPhone. Pages that 404 from your own navigation.

You either have these or you do not. Open your site, click your top five pages, fill out your contact form, and watch the browser inspector. One broken element is one warning sign.

### Sign 7: Customers have started complaining

This is the loudest signal. If you have heard "your website is hard to use" from a paying customer in the last three months, that is the sign. If you have heard it from two different customers, you should already be reading this with a notepad open.

## The scoreboard

Now count. How many of the seven are showing?

### 0 to 1 signs: optimize

You do not need a redesign. You need a punch list. Hire someone for a focused four-to-eight-hour pass. The deliverable is a faster, cleaner version of the site you already have. We cover this kind of work under [unlimited edits and support](/unlimited-edits-and-support/).

### 2 to 3 signs: targeted redesign

Rework the pages where the signs are concentrated. Usually the homepage and one or two service pages. Keep the rest. Budget two to four weeks. See our [pricing](/pricing/) for what a targeted scope looks like at $150/month.

### 4 or more signs: rebuild

A patch job will not hold. The bones of the site are working against you, and every dollar you spend optimizing it is a dollar you will spend twice. Plan for a full redesign on a clean foundation. That is what our [web design](/web-design/) and [web development](/web-development/) services exist to deliver.

The point of the scoreboard is not accuracy to two decimal places. It is discipline. Most small business owners ask for the wrong fix because they describe the symptom, not the cause. The scoreboard makes you describe the cause.

## The cadence rule

A separate question from "do I need a redesign right now?" is "when am I due?"

The honest answer is a cadence, not a number. Major redesign every three to five years. Minor refresh every six to twelve months. Sites that stick to that cadence almost never accumulate four warning signs at once. Sites that do not, almost always do.

If your site is six years old and the only changes since launch have been replacing a logo and adding a holiday banner, treat that age alone as one warning sign. The web is not the site you launched. It moved. Your site stayed.

## Hidden costs nobody quotes you

If the scoreboard tells you to rebuild, the budget conversation is the next obstacle. The number that hurts is rarely the design fee. It is the line items nobody mentions in the proposal.

### Content creation

Real copywriting, not lorem ipsum filled in by you the night before launch. Plan five hundred to three thousand dollars per page if you need help. The seven-section homepage we describe in [our homepage architecture post](/blog/the-seven-homepage-sections/) needs roughly that much copy alone.

### Photography

Stock images of generic professionals shaking hands cost you trust. Real photography of your team and your work runs two hundred to a thousand dollars for a small site, if you do not already have it.

### Monthly maintenance

Updates, hosting, plugin patches if you are on WordPress, content edits as the business changes. Fifty to three hundred dollars a month, ongoing. Our [unlimited edits plan](/unlimited-edits-and-support/) folds this into the same $150/month, which is the case we make in [our DFW comparison post](/blog/comparing-local-dfw-web-design-companies/).

Add these to your design quote before you compare two proposals. The studio that names these costs is more honest than the one that does not.

## How long it actually takes

Traditional agencies will quote three to six months. They are not lying. The process really does take that long when you have account managers, design committees, and a Friday handoff between four different teams.

Streamlined studios deliver the same scope in two to three weeks. <span class="tooltip-term" data-tooltip="Code written by hand in HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, with no page builder or CMS abstraction layer in the way.">Hand-coded</span> studios with content ready can compress it further. The difference is not skill. It is the number of people in the room.

For a five-to-seven-page small business site, a realistic schedule looks like this. Discovery and content audit in week one. Design and copy review in week two. Build and content load in week three. Launch and a thirty-day monitoring window. Anything longer and you are paying for process, not product.
