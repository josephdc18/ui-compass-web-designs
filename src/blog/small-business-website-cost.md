A small business website costs between $200 a year and $50,000.

The spread is not because some agencies are ripping people off. It is because "website" describes four very different products that all happen to share the same word. A 1985 Ford F-150 and a 2026 Lucid Air are both "cars." A Squarespace template and a hand-coded managed site are both "websites." The price difference reflects what the thing actually does.

Here are the four tiers, what each one really gets you, and where the hidden costs hide.

## Tier 1: DIY platform — about $200 a year

This is Wix, Squarespace, Shopify's website builder, Carrd, or any drag-and-drop service. You pick a template, swap in your content, and the platform hosts it. Monthly fee covers the editor, the hosting, the basic SSL certificate, and a handful of integrations.

What it gets you: a site that exists. Usually mobile-responsive. Usually with a basic SEO panel. Usually with a contact form that emails you.

What it does not get you: speed. Page-builder sites typically score in the 50s and 60s on mobile PageSpeed Insights. <a href="/blog/the-1-second-tax/">A one-second delay drops conversions about 7 percent</a>, so the math is brutal. It also does not get you custom design (you are picking from templates), ongoing strategy (the platform does not know your business), or escape velocity (moving the site off the platform later is hard).

The hidden cost is your time. Expect fifteen to thirty hours to get a passable DIY site live. If your time is worth $50 an hour, that is $750 to $1,500 of labor you contributed for free.

When it fits: pre-revenue businesses, side projects, weekend launches, and anyone explicitly choosing speed-to-launch over polish or performance.

## Tier 2: Freelance one-time build — $500 to $3,000

You hire someone on Fiverr, Upwork, or through a referral. They build the site, deliver the files, and you are on your own. Hosting is your problem. Updates are your problem. Bugs found two months later are your problem (or a separate invoice).

What it gets you: a custom-ish site, usually built on WordPress or Squarespace with a paid theme. Sometimes a real design. Almost never on hand-coded infrastructure.

What it does not get you: speed unless you specifically pay for it. Most freelancers at this tier build on WordPress because WordPress is what they know. <a href="/blog/managing-a-website/">Ongoing maintenance</a> is not included. The site will degrade.

The hidden cost is the second invoice. Plugins update and break the site. Themes get deprecated. Images get uploaded oversized. By month six, the site is slower than it was at launch and you do not know why. Fixing it is another quote.

When it fits: businesses that need a one-time project (event microsite, product launch) and have someone in-house to maintain it after launch.

## Tier 3: Local agency project — $5,000 to $15,000 once + hosting

You hire a small DFW or regional agency. They do discovery, design, build, and launch. Project takes six to twelve weeks. Hosting and maintenance are usually a separate monthly fee, between $50 and $200 a month, depending on what is included.

What it gets you: a real custom site. Discovery sessions where someone asks about your business. A design system. Usually built on WordPress, sometimes Webflow, occasionally hand-coded. Real branding. Real photography direction. Usually three months of post-launch support.

What it does not get you: a guarantee that the site will still be fast in year three. Most agency sites are built on WordPress because the business model assumes the client will pay an in-house dev or another agency to maintain it. By year three, the maintenance has lapsed and the site is slow again.

The hidden cost is the rebuild every three to five years. Agency sites have a shelf life that nobody mentions during the sales pitch. Plan for it.

When it fits: businesses doing $500K+ in revenue who can absorb a $10K hit every few years and want a one-shot project rather than an ongoing relationship.

## Tier 4: Specialist managed — $150 to $500 a month, no big upfront

You hire a specialist who builds, hosts, and maintains the site as a continuous service. The work is hand-coded. Hosting is on an edge network with sub-1-second load times. Updates are unlimited. The relationship continues for as long as the business needs the site.

What it gets you: the same custom build the agency tier produces, on faster infrastructure, with no shelf life. Updates whenever you need them. Performance monitored continuously. Accessibility kept in compliance. <a href="/blog/website-is-an-asset/">Site treated as a long-lived business asset</a>, not a one-time project.

What it does not get you: a one-time invoice. The cost is monthly and predictable, which means it shows up on the books every month forever. Some owners find that emotionally harder than a big one-time check even though the math is better.

The hidden cost is: there isn't one. The thing the monthly fee buys is the absence of surprise invoices.

When it fits: most small businesses. The math works at one-to-two new clients per year. <a href="/blog/one-client-pays-for-the-year/">The breakeven post does the numbers.</a>

## How to read a quote

Three questions reveal what tier you are actually buying.

**What happens in month seven?** If the answer is "you pay us another quote," it is a project, not a service. That is fine. Know what you are buying.

**What is the PageSpeed target?** A serious build commits to a number. "We aim for 90+" is real. "Modern and responsive" is marketing. PageSpeed scores are public and free to check at pagespeed.web.dev.

**Is the hosting included? And on what infrastructure?** "Shared hosting" caps your ceiling. <a href="/blog/hosting-decides-your-performance-ceiling/">Hosting decides the speed limit.</a> Premium hosting on an edge network is the difference between PageSpeed 60 and PageSpeed 95.
