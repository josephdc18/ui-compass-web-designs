Getting Your Website Found on Google

Yes, other search engines exist. But Google still owns roughly 90% of all search, so when most people say "SEO" they mean "rank on Google." There's a lot of voodoo around it, but the underlying playbook is real. How well it works depends mostly on how competitive your industry is, plus the occasional outlier of a website that's ranking better than it has any right to.

Google is also a moving target. AI search is the latest shift. The old playbook — write informational content that answers questions — is getting cannibalized by Google's own AI answers, which serve the answer right in the results without sending the searcher to your site. The implication: focus on transactional keywords (the ones with buying intent) and treat informational content as a trust-builder, not a traffic engine.

What Are Keywords?

Keywords are the things people type into Google. "Plumbers in Arlington TX" is a textbook transactional keyword: clear intent, clear location, ready to buy. These are the queries that actually produce revenue for a small business.

Informational keywords (like "how to unblock a shower drain") used to be a great way to pull traffic. They still build trust with the readers who find you that way. But Google's AI now answers most of those questions in the results page itself, which means fewer of those readers ever land on your site. Educational content is still worth writing, but transactional keywords should drive the core SEO strategy.

How We Think About Google

The mental model I use: think of Google as a friend who "knows somebody for everything." That friend will only recommend you when:

  • You can clearly deliver the work, and it's clearly your area of expertise.
  • You're reliable and present yourself professionally.
  • You're well-regarded, especially by other people in your field.

So how does Google translate that into rankings? Three buckets.

On-Page SEO

On-page is the most direct lever. It's the words on your pages, and how clearly they tell Google what you do. Three pieces matter most:

  1. Page Title — the big headline shown in the search results (the SERP). Multi-page websites win here because each page can have its own title — "Plumber in Arlington," "Plumber in Fort Worth," "Plumber in Mansfield" — and you can rank for each city you serve.

  2. Page Description — the line of text shown under the title in the SERP. Roughly 160 characters. Back up the title, work the target keyword in naturally at least once.

  3. Page Content — the actual content people will read. Keyword in your headings, sprinkled naturally through the body. Don't stuff it. The text needs to read like something a human wrote.

Do those three things consistently across your pages and you've told Google exactly what you're good at.

Technical SEO

Technical SEO is how Google reads your site. It's also where the gap between a designer and a developer shows up. The single biggest technical factor is speed.

Think about it this way — if you walked into a business that was slow and sluggish to deal with, what would you assume about the rest of their work? Google thinks the same way about websites.

A properly built website lands strong scores on Google's own tool. Google PageSpeed Insights grades you out of 100 on four metrics:

  • Performance — how fast does your site load?
  • Accessibility — can people with disabilities use it?
  • Best Practices — are you keeping up with modern web standards?
  • SEO — can Google read and interpret your content?

You want 90+ on all four if you're competing for real keywords. GTmetrix is another useful tool — aim for an A. Hit those marks and you keep Google happy and your visitors happy at the same time.

Off-Page SEO

This is where you can really pull rankings up, and it's the hardest part. The mechanism is backlinks — other websites linking to yours. Google reads each link as a vote of confidence, but only if the source is reputable AND relevant. Random links from unrelated sites do nothing.

Think about it. If you were looking for a nail salon, would you trust a recommendation from a carpenter or from a hair stylist? Google works the same way. Backlinks from beauty-industry sites help a beauty business. Backlinks from other trades help a contractor.

This is what builds your domain authority. Google doesn't publish authority scores directly, so third-party tools approximate it. Ahrefs grades domains out of 100. For competitive industries, you'll want 50+. For less competitive niches, 10 to 15 is sometimes enough to rank.

How Much Does SEO Cost?

This mostly depends on how competitive your industry is. If you're competing in a saturated national market, you're looking at thousands per month for backlink campaigns, paid placements, and content production. SEO at that level is a serious investment.

If you're a local business competing against other local businesses — which is most small businesses in DFW — you can often get strong rankings just from our Monthly Subscription, without spending extra on backlinks or paid content. Fast load times, clean structure, transactional copy, and a well-tuned Google Business Profile are usually enough to win the local game.
