Open a Google search for your business. Look at the phone numbers on every result.

Your website's footer says `(901) 490-4750`. Your Google Business Profile shows `+1 901 490 4750`. Yelp has `901-490-4750`. Facebook went with `901.490.4750`. The chamber of commerce listing from 2019 still shows your old number with the area code wrong.

Visually, these are the same number. Algorithmically, Google reads them as different facts about a business it is still trying to figure out. And the map pack — the three results that show on the local search above the blue links — almost always goes to whichever business has the cleanest single answer.

This post is what <span class="tooltip-term" data-tooltip="Name, Address, Phone. The three identity fields Google uses to determine whether multiple listings represent the same business. Mismatches across directories dilute the local-SEO signal.">NAP consistency</span> actually means in 2026, the 30-minute audit, and the one fix that does not trigger a Google review of your listing.

## What Google is trying to do

Local search is fundamentally a data-matching problem. Google has hundreds of mentions of your business across the open web — your site, your GBP, every directory you have ever been listed on, every review platform, every old press mention. Google's job is to merge those mentions into one canonical record so that when someone types "plumber near me," it knows which businesses to surface.

The merge runs on a confidence score. Mentions that agree on all three NAP fields ratchet the score up. Mentions that disagree ratchet it down. Disagreement does not mean "wrong" — it means "uncertain." And uncertainty is a ranking penalty, even if no single field is technically incorrect.

The map pack rewards certainty. The pages of blue links forgive uncertainty. That is why the same business can rank well on regular search and not show up in the map pack at all.

## The three NAP fields, and how each one fails

### Name

The cleanest field to manage, the most-often-mismatched in practice.

A typical small business has variants like:

- The legal name on the license: "UI Compass Web Designs, LLC"
- The trading name on signage: "UI Compass"
- The marketing name on the website: "UI Compass Studio"
- The Facebook page name: "UI Compass • Web Design"
- The Yelp listing: "UI Compass Web Designs"

Each variant adds a small fragment of uncertainty. The fix is to pick one canonical name (whatever is on your GBP listing), use it on every directory, and use it in your <span class="tooltip-term" data-tooltip="Structured data on your website that declares the business name, address, phone, hours, and other identity fields to Google in a machine-readable format.">LocalBusiness schema</span>. The legal name can differ — it is fine for `Doing Business As` records — as long as the trading name is consistent everywhere it is public.

### Address

The most-often-broken field, for reasons that have nothing to do with the owner's attention.

A typical small business address gets written as:

- `123 Main Street, Suite 4` on the website
- `123 Main St Ste 4` on GBP
- `123 Main Street #4` on Yelp
- `123 Main St., Suite #4` on Facebook
- `123 MAIN ST STE 4` on an old chamber of commerce listing

These are five different addresses to Google. Even small things — comma after the street name, period after the abbreviation, capitalization on `STE` — register as different. The fix is to follow [USPS Publication 28](https://pe.usps.com/text/pub28/welcome.htm) standard formatting (no comma, abbreviated suite designator, no `#` sign) and use it everywhere.

### Phone

The field everyone audits and few owners actually fix.

A US phone number can appear as:

- `(901) 490-4750` — most common visible format
- `901-490-4750` — alternate hyphenated
- `901.490.4750` — dot-separated, popular among designers
- `+1 901 490 4750` — international format
- `9014904750` — unformatted

Pick one for the visible display. We recommend `(901) 490-4750` for body text and headers because it reads cleanly. Use the same exact format on every directory. The `tel:` link in your HTML can use a different format (E.164: `+19014904750`) for dialer reliability — see [our tap-to-call post](/blog/tap-to-call-phone-numbers/) for the full breakdown.

## The audit (30 minutes)

### Step 1: Inventory every place your NAP appears

Open a spreadsheet. List every place that publishes your business name, address, or phone. The minimum:

- Your website (homepage, contact page, footer, schema)
- [Google Business Profile](https://www.google.com/business/)
- [Yelp](https://biz.yelp.com/)
- [Facebook business page](https://www.facebook.com/business/)
- [Apple Maps Connect](https://mapsconnect.apple.com/)
- Any industry-specific directories ([Houzz](https://www.houzz.com/), [BBB](https://www.bbb.org/), [Avvo](https://www.avvo.com/), [Healthgrades](https://www.healthgrades.com/))
- Any chamber of commerce or BID listing
- Old press releases or interview transcripts

Most small businesses find 8 to 15 places.

### Step 2: Capture the exact format from each one

For each listing, write down the exact NAP as it appears. Watch for:

- Comma placement
- Suite designator (`Suite` vs `Ste` vs `#`)
- Phone format
- Business name variants (with/without "LLC", with/without "Studio")

### Step 3: Pick a canonical format for each field

The simplest rule: whatever appears on your GBP listing is the canonical version. Match everything else to GBP. If GBP is wrong, fix GBP first (see step 5).

### Step 4: Update every listing

The mechanical part. Open each listing, edit the field, save. For most directories, the change is live in minutes. Facebook and Yelp sometimes require a manual review (24–48 hours).

### Step 5: If GBP needs to change

Edit GBP last, and only if necessary. Name and address changes on GBP can trigger a manual Google review that takes 24 hours to two weeks. Phone changes are usually instant. Plan ahead — do not edit GBP the week before a big campaign.

## What changes after the fix

The map-pack effect compounds over weeks, not days. Two patterns to watch.

### Week 4–8: ranking starts to move

Google re-crawls each listing and updates its confidence score. The map-pack position for your most-competitive query usually moves by week 8. Small dips during the transition are normal — the score recovers and then exceeds the pre-fix baseline.

### Week 12+: review velocity becomes the next lever

Once NAP is clean, the next local-SEO bottleneck is usually review velocity. The full pattern is in [our 5-reviews-a-month post](/blog/five-reviews-a-month-beats-thirty-in-a-week/). NAP consistency is the foundation; review velocity is what builds the visible authority on top of it.

## Why this is foundation-level, not optimization

Most small business owners chase advanced local-SEO tactics (schema, hyperlocal content, citation building) before fixing NAP. That order is backwards. A site with perfect schema and inconsistent NAP is fighting itself — the schema declares one set of facts while the directories declare another, and Google does not know which to believe.

NAP is the foundation. Once it is consistent, every other signal you add — [Review schema](/blog/faq-schema-3x-screen-space/), [Google Business Profile photos](/blog/five-reviews-a-month-beats-thirty-in-a-week/), [hyperlocal blog content](/blog/unblock-ai-crawlers/) — multiplies a clean signal instead of adding to a noisy one.
