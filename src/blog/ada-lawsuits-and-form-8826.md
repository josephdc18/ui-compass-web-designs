Over five thousand ADA web accessibility lawsuits were filed in 2025. Most of them were small businesses.

The plaintiffs are not your customers. They are firms that file at scale, scanning thousands of sites for accessibility violations and sending demand letters to whichever ones look likely to settle. Small businesses settle for the same reason most small businesses do anything — the cost-benefit forces it. Defending a single ADA web case can cost more than the settlement would have, even when the underlying claim is weak.

There is a piece of this most owners do not know about: a federal tax credit specifically designed to offset accessibility remediation costs. It is not a deduction (which lowers taxable income). It is a credit (which lowers the tax bill directly), and it can return up to fifty percent of qualifying spend.

This post is the lawsuit math, the credit math, and why running a free audit today is meaningfully cheaper than waiting for a demand letter.

> **Not legal or tax advice.** Tax credit eligibility depends on your specific situation. Talk to an attorney about your accessibility liability, and talk to your accountant about Form 8826 before claiming the credit.

## The lawsuit landscape

ADA web lawsuit volume has climbed every year since 2017. The plaintiffs' bar that specializes in this work has grown alongside it. The pattern is consistent:

- The plaintiff (or their attorney) runs an automated accessibility scan on hundreds or thousands of websites.
- Sites that fail enough criteria are flagged.
- A <span class="tooltip-term" data-tooltip="A letter from a plaintiff's attorney citing specific accessibility failures on your site and demanding you fix them, pay a settlement, and cover attorney fees. The first step in most small business ADA web cases.">demand letter</span> goes to the business, citing specific failures and asking for a settlement plus attorney fees.
- Most small businesses settle within 30 to 60 days, in the **$5,000 to $25,000 range** plus the plaintiff's attorney fees on top.

That is the per-case cost. Some businesses have received multiple demand letters from different plaintiffs over successive years. The site does not "stay fixed" if the underlying code never gets remediated.

## Why small businesses are the easy target

Two structural reasons.

### Lower legal sophistication

Large enterprises have in-house counsel, retained outside firms, and policies for handling demand letters. They negotiate. They occasionally fight. Small businesses receive the letter, read it, and call their general-counsel attorney who has never seen an ADA web case before. The plaintiff's attorney knows this. The leverage is one-sided.

### Predictable settlement math

A small business owner can do the cost-benefit in five minutes. Pay $7,000 to settle, or pay $15,000 to defend and probably still pay something to settle. The settle path looks rational. The plaintiff's economic model depends on owners doing that math the same way, every time.

The aggregate effect: a transfer of small amounts of money from many small businesses to a small number of firms that file accessibility lawsuits at scale.

## What your site costs you if you ignore it

The realistic exposure picture for a typical small business in 2026.

- **Probability of receiving a demand letter in any given year**: low but rising. Industry-specific (legal, healthcare, e-commerce are higher risk). Geographic (California and New York lead by a wide margin, but Texas and Florida have climbed).
- **Probability of settling if you do receive one**: well above 80%, based on what we hear from clients and the published court data.
- **Expected cost of settling**: $5,000 to $25,000 plus the plaintiff's attorney fees. Median is roughly $8,000 to $12,000.
- **Probability of a second demand letter if you fix nothing**: rises sharply, because the underlying site is still failing the same scan.

A site that fails [the three basic WCAG tests](/blog/wcag-2-2-aa-in-five-minutes/) is exposed to all of the above. A site that passes is statistically very unlikely to be a target.

## The IRS Disabled Access Credit (Form 8826)

The federal government has had a tax credit on the books since 1990 specifically designed to offset accessibility remediation costs for small businesses. Most owners have never heard of it.

### What the credit does

[Form 8826](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8826) lets eligible small businesses claim a <span class="tooltip-term" data-tooltip="A reduction in your tax bill applied dollar-for-dollar after your tax is calculated. Different from a deduction, which only reduces the income that is taxed.">tax credit</span> equal to **50% of eligible accessibility expenses** between $250 and $10,250 in a given tax year. The maximum credit is $5,000 per year — that is, 50% of $10,000, which is the spending range after subtracting the $250 floor.

The credit can be claimed every year you have qualifying expenses. There is no lifetime cap.

### Who is eligible

To qualify, your business must meet either of the following in the prior tax year:

- **30 or fewer full-time employees**, or
- **$1 million or less in gross receipts**.

You only need to meet one of the two thresholds. Most small businesses we work with meet both easily.

### What kind of spend counts

Accessibility-related professional services, audits, code remediation, training, and ongoing maintenance generally qualify. The IRS framing is "expenses incurred to comply with the ADA" — which covers website remediation when the work brings the site closer to recognized accessibility standards (WCAG is the de-facto courtroom benchmark).

What probably does not count: overlay-widget subscriptions, since those do not provide durable accessibility improvements. We covered the overlay problem in [our overlay-widget post](/blog/why-accessibility-overlay-widgets-get-sued/).

The exact eligibility rules depend on your filing situation — ask your accountant.

## The math, end to end

Walk through a realistic small business case.

### Scenario: a 4-employee service business in DFW with a 6-page site

- Site fails [a WAVE scan](/blog/wcag-2-2-aa-in-five-minutes/) with 35 accessibility issues.
- Full remediation quote: $3,500. Includes audit, code-level fixes, alt text, form labels, ongoing quarterly review for one year.

### Without the credit

Out-of-pocket cost: $3,500. Annual budget impact: $3,500.

### With the Disabled Access Credit

Eligible expenses: $3,500. Subtract the $250 floor: $3,250 in qualifying spend. Credit: 50% of $3,250 = **$1,625**.

Net out-of-pocket after credit: $3,500 - $1,625 = **$1,875**.

### Compare to the alternative

Doing nothing keeps the exposure where it is. A single demand letter at the median settlement of $8,000 — plus the plaintiff's attorney fees, plus your own attorney time on the response — easily clears $10,000. And you still have to fix the site after the settlement, or risk a second letter.

The net math is one-sided. The fix at $1,875 net is roughly an order of magnitude cheaper than a single settlement, and the fix is durable. The settlement is not.

## What to actually do

A short list, in the order most likely to make money rather than cost it.

### Step 1: Run the free audit yourself (5 minutes, $0)

[The three tests in our WCAG 2.2 AA post](/blog/wcag-2-2-aa-in-five-minutes/). WAVE scan, keyboard navigation, alt-text audit. The output is a count of how many issues your site is currently flagged for.

### Step 2: Scope the real fix

If WAVE finds more than 5 issues, the gap is real. Get a quote from a developer who works in accessibility. The remediation pass for a typical small business site is usually 8 to 24 hours of focused work. Quoted cost: $1,500 to $4,000.

### Step 3: Document the spend

Keep the invoice, the audit reports, the before-and-after WAVE scans. Your accountant needs this paperwork to claim the credit on Form 8826.

### Step 4: Schedule the quarterly review

Sites drift back into accessibility failure after content edits, plugin updates, and theme changes. A quarterly pass keeps the gap from re-opening. The ongoing work also generally qualifies for the credit in subsequent years.

### Step 5: Talk to your accountant before filing

The credit is real, but the rules are specific. Your accountant will confirm which expenses qualify, attach Form 8826 to your tax return, and reconcile the credit against any other accessibility-related deductions you might be taking.
